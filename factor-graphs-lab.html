Anatomy of a factor graph

A robot moves through three poses \(p_1, p_2, p_3\) and takes bearing measurements to two landmarks \(\ell_1, \ell_2\). One absolute measurement of \(p_1\) pins the whole thing to the world. Without it, every measurement would be relative and the solution could slide around freely. The unknown state is just everything stacked:

\[ x = \begin{bmatrix} p_1 & p_2 & p_3 & \ell_1 & \ell_2 \end{bmatrix}^\top \]
(1.2)

We want the posterior \(p(x\mid z)\). Bayes' law, plus the assumption that the robot's motion is a Markov chain, factors it into small pieces. Each piece depends on only a few variables:

\[ \begin{aligned} p(x\mid z) \propto\;& \underbrace{p(p_1)\,p(p_2\mid p_1)\,p(p_3\mid p_2)}_{\text{motion prior}}\;\underbrace{p(\ell_1)\,p(\ell_2)}_{\text{landmark priors}} \\ \times\;& \underbrace{p(z_1\mid p_1)}_{\text{absolute}}\;\underbrace{p(z_2\mid p_1,\ell_1)\,p(z_3\mid p_2,\ell_1)\,p(z_4\mid p_3,\ell_2)}_{\text{bearings}} \end{aligned} \]
(1.4)

A factor graph draws exactly this product. It has a node for every unknown and a small square for every factor. A factor connects only to the variables it is a function of. Measurements are not nodes: they are known, so they are baked into the factors.

IntuitionThink of each factor as a spring attached to the variables it touches. Its rest length is the measurement and its stiffness is \(1/\sigma^2\). The MAP estimate is the configuration where the total spring energy is smallest. That energy is exactly the least-squares cost you'll meet in §1.2.

RememberA factor graph is a bipartite graph \(F=(\mathcal U,\mathcal V,\mathcal E)\) that represents \(\phi(x)=\prod_i \phi_i(x_i)\) (1.7). A factor's values only need to be proportional to the density it came from. Constants that don't depend on \(x\) can be dropped.

One language, many SLAM problems

Factor graphs are the lingua franca across SLAM variants. Teams that disagree about sensors can still agree on the graph. Each factor is “an equation involving the variables it is connected to”. There are usually many more equations than unknowns, which is why we need noise models to weigh them.

From MAP inference to least squares

The MAP estimate is the single most probable state. Dividing by \(p(z)\) doesn't change where the maximum is, so we drop it:

\[ x^{\text{MAP}} = \arg\max_x p(x\mid z) = \arg\max_x p(z\mid x)\,p(x) = \arg\max_x \prod_i \phi_i(x_i) \]
(1.6, 1.8)

Now assume every factor is Gaussian in its measurement error. Here \(h_i\) predicts what the sensor should read given the state, and \(\Sigma_i\) is the noise covariance:

\[ \phi_i(x_i) \propto \exp\!\Big(-\tfrac12 \lVert z_i - h_i(x_i)\rVert^2_{\Sigma_i}\Big) \]
(1.17)

Take \(-\log\). The product becomes a sum, \(\exp\) disappears, and maximizing becomes minimizing. Drop the \(\tfrac12\):

\[ x^{\text{MAP}} = \arg\min_x \sum_i \lVert z_i - h_i(x_i)\rVert^2_{\Sigma_i} \]
(1.18)

That's the whole trick. Probabilistic inference on a factor graph becomes nonlinear least squares. The instrument below shows both views at once for a single unknown scalar \(x\). The top plot shows each factor and their product. The bottom plot shows the same thing after \(-\log\): each factor is a cost curve and the curves add up.

Product of factors ⇔ sum of costseq 1.8 ⇔ 1.18
Try this
  1. Two sensors: make one σ smaller. The posterior slides toward the more confident sensor. It's the information-weighted mean \(x^\star = \frac{\sum \mu_i/\sigma_i^2}{\sum 1/\sigma_i^2}\), and its width is \(1/\sigma_\star^2 = \sum 1/\sigma_i^2\). Information adds.
  2. Look at the bottom plot. Each Gaussian factor is a parabola, and the sum of parabolas is a parabola. That's why linear-Gaussian problems are solved in one step.
  3. Outlier: one measurement is badly wrong and drags the Gaussian MAP away from the truth. Change that factor's model to Cauchy or Huber. Its cost flattens out far from the mean, so the outlier stops pulling. This is the idea behind robust factors (Chapter 3).
  4. With a Cauchy factor in the mix the total cost is no longer a parabola, and it can even have two minima. Robustness costs you convexity.
RememberFor Gaussian factors, \(\text{factor} = \exp(-\tfrac12\cdot\text{cost})\). Every “probability” statement in this chapter has a “least squares” twin. Product ↔ sum. Max ↔ min. Information \(1/\sigma^2\) ↔ weight.

Mahalanobis distance and whitening

The squared Mahalanobis distance \(\lVert e\rVert^2_\Sigma \triangleq e^\top \Sigma^{-1} e\) (1.10) measures an error in units of its own uncertainty. A 10 cm error is tiny for a GPS but huge for a laser. Because \(\Sigma^{-1} = \Sigma^{-\top/2}\Sigma^{-1/2}\), you can turn it into an ordinary Euclidean norm:

\[ \lVert e\rVert^2_\Sigma = e^\top\Sigma^{-1}e = \lVert \Sigma^{-1/2} e\rVert_2^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad A_i = \Sigma_i^{-1/2}H_i,\;\; b_i = \Sigma_i^{-1/2}\big(z_i - h_i(x_i^0)\big) \]
(1.22–1.23)

This is whitening. It stretches space so the uncertainty ellipse becomes a unit circle. Afterwards every row of \(A\) is unitless and all factors can share one cost function. For a scalar measurement it just means dividing by \(\sigma\).

What a single factor knows

The book makes an “important and non-obvious observation”: on its own, a factor usually describes an under-specified density. A bearing measurement \(h(p,\ell)=\operatorname{atan2}(\ell_y-p_y,\ell_x-p_x)\) (1.13) is one number, but a landmark has two coordinates. One bearing says “somewhere along this ray”. A range says “somewhere on this circle”. Only the product of factors pins the landmark down.

Try this
  1. Leave only one bearing on. The likelihood is a ray: \(\Lambda\) for the landmark has rank 1 and one eigenvalue is ~0. In the book's toy, \(\ell_2\) is seen only once, from \(p_3\). This is why the book adds the priors \(\phi_4, \phi_5\).
  2. Turn on a second bearing from a different pose. The rays intersect and the blob is well defined. Now move the two sensors close together. The rays become nearly parallel and the ellipse stretches along them. This is weak geometry.
  3. Use two ranges. Two circles meet at two points, and the likelihood has two peaks. That's a non-convex cost, and it's why nonlinear SLAM needs a good initial guess (p. 29).
  4. Combine one bearing and one range from the same pose. Ray × circle gives a single blob. That's why range-bearing sensors are so convenient.

The factor graph lab

This is the core instrument: a real 2D SLAM back-end. Poses are treated as 2D positions, as the book does in this chapter; rotations get proper treatment in Chapter 2. Each iteration it linearizes every factor around the current estimate (1.19–1.20), whitens it (1.23), stacks everything into one sparse system \(A\delta \approx b\) (1.24) and solves the normal equations with Cholesky (1.25–1.28):

\[ \underbrace{A^\top A}_{\Lambda = R^\top R}\,\delta^* = A^\top b \quad\Longrightarrow\quad R^\top y = A^\top b,\;\; R\,\delta^* = y \]
(1.25–1.28)

Build your own graph, or start from the book's toy. Click a factor or a variable to inspect its Jacobian blocks, residual and uncertainty.

  3. Gauge freedom. Delete both unary factors on \(p_1\) (φ1 and φ6). With only relative measurements \(\Lambda\) becomes singular (or nearly so, kept alive only by the weak landmark priors). Cholesky fails or the ellipses explode. This is why the book anchors \(p_1\).
  4. Linear means one step. Load the pose graph. Odometry \(p_{t+1}-p_t\) is linear in position (1.16), so a single Gauss-Newton step lands exactly on the optimum and the next step changes nothing. Bearings and ranges are nonlinear, so the toy needs several.
  5. Loop closure. In the pose graph, delete the loop closure factor, run again, and watch the ellipses grow along the trajectory. Add it back with + Factor → odometry, clicking the last pose and then \(p_1\). Uncertainty collapses everywhere.
  6. Break Gauss-Newton. Press Perturb a few times on the bearing toy, then use GN. Sometimes the cost goes up. LM rejects such steps and raises λ, and dogleg shrinks its trust region (see the cost plot: hollow dots are rejected attempts).
RememberEach iteration solves a linear least-squares problem \(\min_\delta\lVert A\delta-b\rVert^2\) (1.24). \(A\) has one block row per factor and one block column per variable. \(\Lambda = A^\top A\) has a non-zero block \((i,j)\) exactly when variables \(i\) and \(j\) share a factor. \(\Sigma = \Lambda^{-1}\) gives the uncertainty (the ellipses), and it is usually dense, which is why we never form it in large problems.

Steepest descent, Gauss-Newton, Levenberg-Marquardt, dogleg

All four methods solve a series of local quadratic models of \(J(x)=\sum_i\lVert z_i-h_i(x_i)\rVert^2_{\Sigma_i}\) (1.34). They differ in how far they trust the model:

\[ \delta_{sd} = \alpha\,A^\top b \qquad A^\top A\,\delta_{gn} = A^\top b \qquad \big(A^\top A + \lambda\,\mathrm{diag}(A^\top A)\big)\delta_{lm} = A^\top b \]
(1.35, 1.36, 1.38)

Steepest descent follows the negative gradient \(-\nabla J = 2A^\top b\). It's safe but slow near the minimum. Gauss-Newton jumps to the minimum of the quadratic model, which is fast when the model is good and can diverge when it isn't. LM blends the two through λ: λ = 0 gives GN, and a large λ gives small gradient steps. Dogleg computes the SD and GN steps separately and bends between them inside a trust region of radius Δ. It updates Δ using the gain ratio \(\rho = \frac{J(x)-J(x+\delta)}{L(0)-L(\delta)}\) (1.39), where \(L(\delta)=\lVert A\delta-b\rVert^2\) is the quadratic model (the book writes its gradient).

Factor graphs are sparsity patterns

Real SLAM problems have thousands to millions of unknowns, but each measurement touches only one or two of them. The odometry chain over 100 poses has 100 binary factors, not \(\binom{100}{2}\). Landmarks are each seen from a handful of poses. And there are no factors between landmarks at all. This sparsity carries straight through to \(A\), to \(\Lambda\), and to the Cholesky factor \(R\).

But \(R\) can be denser than \(\Lambda\). Factorization creates fill-in, and how much depends entirely on the variable ordering. Every ordering gives the same solution, but the cost can differ enormously. Finding the minimum-fill ordering is NP-hard [1239], so solvers use heuristics like COLAMD [241].

Variable elimination: factor graph → Bayes net

Here is the deepest idea in the chapter. Pick an ordering. For each variable \(x_j\) in turn, remove all factors touching it and multiply them into a product factor \(\psi(x_j, s_j)\), where the separator \(s_j\) is every other variable those factors touch. Then split it in two:

\[ \psi(x_j, s_j) = p(x_j \mid s_j)\;\tau(s_j) \]
(1.43)

Keep the conditional \(p(x_j\mid s_j)\) and put the new factor \(\tau(s_j)\) back into the graph. When you're done, the factor graph has become a Bayes net \(p(x)=\prod_j p(x_j\mid s_j)\) (1.42). In the linear-Gaussian case, each step is one step of partial QR on the stacked block rows (1.48). The rows \([R_j\;T_j]\) become the block row of the upper-triangular factor \(R\), and \(\tilde A_\tau\) is the new factor. A Bayes net is an upper-triangular matrix.

After step 1 you should see fig 1.11: \(\phi_4,\phi_7,\phi_8\) are consumed, the row \([R_1\;T_{13}\;T_{14}]\) appears, and a red \(\tau(p_1,p_2)\) with block row \(\tilde A_{13}\,\tilde A_{14}\) is added.   2. Choose “p₂ first”. Eliminating \(p_2\) touches \(p_1, p_3, \ell_1\) and creates a 3-way factor. That's fill-in: \(R\) gets blocks where \(\Lambda\) had zeros.
  3. On the star, eliminate the landmark first. Its five poses become one big clique, and every remaining step is dense. Eliminate the poses first instead: no fill at all. Same answer, very different cost.
  4. On the loop, any ordering must create some fill. A cycle can't be eliminated without adding a chord. That chordal structure is what makes the Bayes tree possible (§1.7).
RememberSolving is back-substitution in reverse elimination order. The last variable has no parents, so \(x_n^* = R_n^{-1}d_n\). Every earlier variable then uses the already-known separator: \(x_j^* = R_j^{-1}(d_j - T_j s_j^*)\) (1.53). Each conditional is a Gaussian with mean \(R_j^{-1}(d_j-T_js_j)\) and covariance \((R_j^\top R_j)^{-1}\) (1.52).

The Bayes tree and incremental smoothing (iSAM2)

A Bayes net produced by elimination is chordal: every cycle longer than three has a chord. That means its cliques can be arranged in a tree. Each node of the Bayes tree is a clique \(c_k = f_k : s_k\), with frontal variables \(f_k\) and separator \(s_k\) (the overlap with the parent), and it stores one conditional \(p(f_k\mid s_k)\):

\[ p(x) = \prod_k p(f_k \mid s_k) \]
(1.55)

Information flows upward to the root during elimination. So when a new factor arrives, only the cliques on the paths from its variables to the root change. iSAM2 detaches that top part, turns it back into a factor graph, adds the new factor, re-eliminates, and reattaches the untouched sub-trees (the “orphans”).

RememberiSAM2 [534] = Bayes tree + local re-elimination + selective re-linearization. Only factors whose variables have moved more than a threshold get re-linearized, and back-substitution stops early once changes are small. It's implemented in GTSAM as gtsam.ISAM2.

The toy example in GTSAM

The chapter points to GTSAM (github.com/borglab/gtsam), written by the chapter's authors. Its planar SLAM example has the same topology as fig 1.3: \(\ell_1\) seen from \(x_1,x_2\) and \(\ell_2\) from \(x_3\). It uses full \(SE(2)\) poses and range-bearing factors. Run it with pip install gtsam.

import numpy as np
import gtsam
from gtsam.symbol_shorthand import L, X

graph = gtsam.NonlinearFactorGraph()

# Noise models: Sigmas() builds a diagonal Σ; GTSAM whitens with Σ^(-1/2) for you (eq 1.23)
PRIOR_NOISE = gtsam.noiseModel.Diagonal.Sigmas(np.array([0.3, 0.3, 0.1]))
ODOM_NOISE  = gtsam.noiseModel.Diagonal.Sigmas(np.array([0.2, 0.2, 0.1]))
MEAS_NOISE  = gtsam.noiseModel.Diagonal.Sigmas(np.array([0.1, 0.2]))   # bearing, range

# φ1: prior on the first pose (anchors the gauge)
graph.add(gtsam.PriorFactorPose2(X(1), gtsam.Pose2(0.0, 0.0, 0.0), PRIOR_NOISE))
# φ2, φ3: odometry (binary "between" factors)
graph.add(gtsam.BetweenFactorPose2(X(1), X(2), gtsam.Pose2(2.0, 0.0, 0.0), ODOM_NOISE))
graph.add(gtsam.BetweenFactorPose2(X(2), X(3), gtsam.Pose2(2.0, 0.0, 0.0), ODOM_NOISE))
# φ7, φ8, φ9: landmark measurements
graph.add(gtsam.BearingRangeFactor2D(X(1), L(1), gtsam.Rot2.fromDegrees(45), np.sqrt(8.0), MEAS_NOISE))
graph.add(gtsam.BearingRangeFactor2D(X(2), L(1), gtsam.Rot2.fromDegrees(90), 2.0, MEAS_NOISE))
graph.add(gtsam.BearingRangeFactor2D(X(3), L(2), gtsam.Rot2.fromDegrees(90), 2.0, MEAS_NOISE))

# Initial guess x0: the linearization point of eq 1.19
initial = gtsam.Values()
initial.insert(X(1), gtsam.Pose2(-0.25, 0.20, 0.15))
initial.insert(X(2), gtsam.Pose2(2.30, 0.10, -0.20))
initial.insert(X(3), gtsam.Pose2(4.10, 0.10, 0.10))
initial.insert(L(1), gtsam.Point2(1.80, 2.10))
initial.insert(L(2), gtsam.Point2(4.10, 1.80))

# §1.4: Levenberg-Marquardt (swap in GaussNewtonOptimizer or DoglegOptimizer)
result = gtsam.LevenbergMarquardtOptimizer(graph, initial).optimize()
print(result)

# Σ = Λ⁻¹ blocks: the 95% ellipses in the lab
marginals = gtsam.Marginals(graph, result)
print(marginals.marginalCovariance(L(2)))   # long and thin, like in the lab

# §1.7: the same graph, incrementally, with iSAM2
isam = gtsam.ISAM2()
isam.update(graph, initial)
print(isam.calculateEstimate())

A–ZCheat sheet

Cheat sheet

Factor \(\phi_i(x_i)\)
A non-negative function of a few variables, proportional to a prior or a measurement likelihood. In Gaussian form: \(\exp(-\tfrac12\lVert z_i-h_i(x_i)\rVert^2_{\Sigma_i})\) (1.17).
Factor graph
Bipartite graph of factor nodes and variable nodes. Edges only go between a factor and the variables it depends on. It represents \(\phi(x)=\prod_i\phi_i(x_i)\) (1.7).
Unary / binary
A factor on one variable (priors, GPS) or two variables (odometry, landmark measurements).
MAP estimate
\(\arg\max_x p(x\mid z)\). With Gaussian factors it equals \(\arg\min_x\sum_i\lVert z_i-h_i(x_i)\rVert^2_{\Sigma_i}\) (1.18).
Mahalanobis norm
\(\lVert e\rVert^2_\Sigma = e^\top\Sigma^{-1}e\) (1.10). Error measured in units of its own standard deviation.
Whitening
Multiply by \(\Sigma^{-1/2}\) so every residual is unitless with identity covariance: \(A_i=\Sigma_i^{-1/2}H_i\), \(b_i=\Sigma_i^{-1/2}(z_i-h_i(x^0_i))\) (1.23).
Measurement Jacobian \(A\)
Stacked whitened Jacobians. One block row per factor, one block column per variable. Its block pattern is the factor graph (fig 1.7).
Information matrix \(\Lambda\)
\(A^\top A\), the Gauss-Newton Hessian. Block \((i,j)\) is non-zero iff \(x_i, x_j\) share a factor (fig 1.8). Its inverse is the covariance.
Cholesky / QR
\(\Lambda = R^\top R\) or \(A = Q\begin{bmatrix}R\\0\end{bmatrix}\). Both give the same upper-triangular \(R\) up to signs. Then solve by forward and back substitution (1.25–1.33).
Fill-in
Non-zeros in \(R\) where \(\Lambda\) had zeros. Controlled by the variable ordering. Minimizing it exactly is NP-hard, so solvers use COLAMD.
Gauss-Newton
Solve \(A^\top A\,\delta = A^\top b\) and step. Quadratic convergence near the optimum, but can diverge from a poor guess.
Levenberg-Marquardt
\((A^\top A+\lambda\,\mathrm{diag}(A^\top A))\delta=A^\top b\). Reject steps that raise the cost and multiply λ by 10. Accept and divide by 10.
Powell's dogleg
Combine the SD (Cauchy) and GN steps inside a trust region Δ. Adapt Δ using the gain ratio ρ (1.39). Needs one factorization per accepted step.
Gauge freedom
With only relative measurements the cost is unchanged by a global shift, so \(\Lambda\) is singular. A prior on one pose removes it.
Variable elimination
For each \(x_j\): multiply adjacent factors into \(\psi(x_j,s_j)\), then factor it as \(p(x_j\mid s_j)\tau(s_j)\) (1.43). The result is a Bayes net, and in the Gaussian case this is sparse QR or Cholesky.
Separator \(s_j\)
Variables adjacent to \(x_j\) when it is eliminated. Big separators mean fill-in and expensive steps.
Bayes net
A DAG of conditionals \(p(x_j\mid s_j)\). Its structure is the sparsity of the upper-triangular \(R\).
Chordal graph
Every cycle of length > 3 has a chord. Elimination always produces one, which is why its cliques form a tree.
Bayes tree
A directed tree of cliques \(f_k : s_k\), each holding \(p(f_k\mid s_k)\) (1.55). It's the data structure behind iSAM2.
iSAM2
Incremental smoothing and mapping. A new factor affects only the paths from its variables to the root. Re-eliminate that part, reattach orphans, and re-linearize selectively.
