Rotations are not vectors. Time is not a list.

Chapter 1 quietly simplified two things. It treated every unknown as a plain list of numbers you can add, and it treated time as a row of separate snapshots. Chapter 2 fixes both. This page walks through the fixes with instruments you can drag, running the chapter's real math.

0The big picture

Two fixes, one idea each

The chapter has two halves. They look different, but each one rests on a single idea. Once you hold these two ideas, every equation in the chapter is a detail.

In plain words

Part A (manifolds). Rotations live on a curved surface, so you can't just add to them. Instead, do your math on a small flat sheet that touches the surface where you currently are (the tangent space). Then wrap the answer back onto the surface with a function called Exp.

Part B (continuous time). Instead of one unknown pose per measurement, describe the whole trajectory as a smooth curve controlled by a few knobs. Each measurement touches only the knobs near its time stamp, so the factor graph stays small and sparse.

Why a rotation is not a vector

In plain words

A vector is something you can add and scale: 2 m + 3 m = 5 m. Every optimizer in Chapter 1 depended on that: it updated the guess with \(x \leftarrow x + \delta\).

A rotation matrix doesn't work like that. To be a real rotation, it must keep all lengths and angles the same (that's \(R^\top R = I\)) and must not mirror the world (that's \(\det R = 1\)). Add two rotation matrices, or nudge a single entry, and you get a matrix that stretches or skews shapes. It is no longer a rotation.

In the plane, a rotation by angle \(\theta\) is

\[ R = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix} \in SO(2), \qquad R^\top R = I,\;\; \det R = 1 \]
(2.1)

The set of all such matrices is called \(SO(d)\), the special orthogonal group. It has 4 numbers but only one degree of freedom, \(\theta\). In 3D it has 9 numbers but only three degrees of freedom (think roll, pitch, yaw). The other numbers are tied together by the constraints. Try breaking one below.

  1. Compose: whatever angles you pick, the square keeps its shape and \(M^\top M = I\). Multiplying two rotations always gives a rotation, by \(\theta_1+\theta_2\). The book calls this closure (p. 55). It's the one operation rotations are happy with.

  3. Nudge: this is what Gauss-Newton would do if you made the 4 entries of \(R\) your unknowns and stepped \(R + \delta\). Even small δ turns the square into a parallelogram. Poses: one matrix that turns and moves

In plain words

A pose is “where something is” plus “which way it faces”. Pack the rotation \(R\) and the translation \(t\) into one bigger matrix \(T\). Then one matrix multiplication moves a point from one coordinate frame to another, and one multiplication chains two poses together.

The trick is to append a 1 to every point (a homogeneous point). That extra 1 is what lets the translation part act during the multiplication.

\[ T_{ba} = \begin{bmatrix} R_{ba} & t_{ba} \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \in SE(d), \qquad \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \ell_b \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\tilde\ell_b} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} R_{ba} & t_{ba} \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{T_{ba}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \ell_a \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\tilde\ell_a} \]
(2.2, 2.3)

Read the subscripts right to left: \(T_{ba}\) takes coordinates in frame a and gives coordinates in frame b. A common trip-up: the pose of the robot frame \(b\) as seen from \(a\) is \(T_{ab}\), and the matrix that converts coordinates from \(a\) into \(b\) is its inverse, \(T_{ba} = T_{ab}^{-1}\). The instrument shows both.

Being a group is what lets us “undo” and “chain” motions. Being a smooth surface (a manifold) is what lets us take derivatives on it.

A curved space and its flat tangent space

In plain words

The set of all rotations is a curved space, a manifold. The Earth's surface is the everyday example: it's curved, but near any town it looks flat, so a flat paper map works fine locally.

That flat map is the tangent space \(T_\chi\mathcal M\) at your current point \(\chi\). On it, ordinary vector math works again: you can add, scale, and take derivatives. To use a step you computed on the flat map, you must bring it back onto the curved surface. That “bring back” step is called a retraction. The most natural one is the exponential map, Exp: walk the length of your step along the surface, in the direction of your step.

The circle is the simplest manifold. A unit complex number \(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta\) is a 2D rotation, so \(SO(2)\) is a circle. The sphere is the book's Figure 2.1. In both, compare three ways to apply a step \(\xi\) to the point \(\chi\).

Exp and Log: the bridge between vectors and rotations

In plain words

Every matrix Lie group comes with a partner: a plain vector space with exactly as many entries as the group has degrees of freedom (1 for \(SO(2)\), 3 for \(SO(3)\) and \(SE(2)\), 6 for \(SE(3)\)). That vector space is the tangent space at the identity, and it's called the Lie algebra.

There are two steps from a vector to a rotation. First the hat \((\cdot)^\wedge\) arranges the vector's numbers into a special matrix. Then the matrix exponential \(\exp\) turns that matrix into a group element. Going back uses \(\log\), then the vee \((\cdot)^\vee\). The book writes the two-step shortcuts with capitals: \(\operatorname{Exp}(\theta) = \exp(\theta^\wedge)\) and \(\operatorname{Log}(R) = \log(R)^\vee\).

The map of operators (click any arrow or box)fig 2.2
Click a box or an arrow.

For \(SO(2)\), the hat turns one number into a 2 × 2 skew matrix, and the infinite series of the matrix exponential adds up to exactly the rotation matrix from (2.1):

\[ \theta^\wedge = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -\theta \\ \theta & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \operatorname{Exp}(\theta) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n!}(\theta^\wedge)^n = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix} \]
(2.4–2.5)

For \(SO(3)\), the hat is the skew-symmetric (cross-product) matrix. The vector \(\theta\) is an axis-angle vector: its direction is the rotation axis and its length is the angle in radians.

\[ \theta^\wedge = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -\theta_3 & \theta_2 \\ \theta_3 & 0 & -\theta_1 \\ -\theta_2 & \theta_1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \in so(3), \qquad R = \operatorname{Exp}(\theta) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}(\theta^\wedge)^n \in SO(3) \]
(2.6)

The series has a closed form, Rodrigues' formula: with \(\phi = \lVert\theta\rVert\) and \(a = \theta/\phi\), \(R = \cos\phi\, I + (1-\cos\phi)\, a a^\top + \sin\phi\, a^\wedge\). The instrument computes both and compares them.

Poses work the same way. For \(SE(d)\), the vector \(\xi = (\rho, \theta)\) has a translation-like part \(\rho\) and a rotation part \(\theta\), and the hat builds a \((d+1)\times(d+1)\) matrix:

\[ T = \operatorname{Exp}(\xi) = \exp(\xi^\wedge), \qquad \xi^\wedge = \begin{bmatrix} \theta^\wedge & \rho \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \xi = \begin{bmatrix} \rho \\ \theta \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb R^{3(d-1)} \]
(2.7)

Optimizing a pose: perturb in the tangent space

In plain words

We can't update a pose with \(T + \delta\). Instead, we update it with \(T \oplus \xi = T\,\operatorname{Exp}(\xi)\): keep the current guess, then apply a small extra motion \(\xi\) that lives on the flat tangent space. \(\xi\) is an ordinary vector with exactly 3 numbers in 2D (6 in 3D), so all of Chapter 1 applies to it unchanged: linearize, build \(A\delta\approx b\), solve.

Once you have the best step \(\xi^*\), wrap it on with \(T \leftarrow T\,\operatorname{Exp}(\xi^*)\). The product of two poses is always a pose (closure), so the guess can never become an invalid matrix. Repeat until \(\xi^*\) is tiny.

The book's example is perspective-n-point (PnP): known landmarks \(\tilde\ell_{w,i}\) in the world, a camera with unknown pose \(T_{cw}\), and pixel measurements \(z_i\):

\[ z_i = h_i\!\left(T_{cw}\,\tilde\ell_{w,i}\right) + \eta_i, \qquad T^*_{cw} = \arg\min_{T_{cw}} \sum_i \big\lVert z_i - h_i(T_{cw}\tilde\ell_{w,i}) \big\rVert^2_{\Sigma_i} \]
(2.8–2.9)

Perturb the guess \(T^0_{cw}\) on the right, then keep only the first-order term of the exponential series:

\[ \begin{aligned} T_{cw} &= T^0_{cw}\operatorname{Exp}(\xi) \approx T^0_{cw}(I + \xi^\wedge) \\ T_{cw}\tilde\ell &\approx T^0_{cw}\tilde\ell + T^0_{cw}\,\tilde\ell^{\odot}\,\xi, \qquad \tilde\ell^{\odot} = \begin{bmatrix} I & -\ell^\wedge \\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} \]
(2.10–2.15)

The \(\odot\) operator just rearranges \(\xi^\wedge\tilde\ell\) into “matrix × \(\xi\)”, so the unknown sits on the right where least squares wants it. Chain it with the sensor model's own Jacobian and you get the familiar linear factor, now in \(\xi\):

\[ \begin{aligned} H_i &= \frac{\partial h_i}{\partial \ell}\Big|_{T^0\tilde\ell}\; T^0_{cw}\,\tilde\ell^{\odot}_{w,i} && \text{(chain rule)} \\ &\big\lVert z_i - h_i(T^0_{cw}\tilde\ell_{w,i}) - H_i\,\xi \big\rVert^2_{\Sigma_i} && \text{(linear factor in } \xi) \\ T^0_{cw} &\leftarrow T^0_{cw}\oplus\xi^* && \text{(update)} \end{aligned} \]
(2.16, 2.18, 2.19)

Below is a 2D version of the same problem. In 2D, \(\xi = (\rho_1,\rho_2,\theta)\), \(\theta^\wedge\) is \(\theta J\) with \(J = \begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}\), and \(\tilde\ell^\odot = \begin{bmatrix} I & J\ell \\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}\). The sensor sees landmarks either as points in its own frame (like a lidar) or as bearings only (like a 1D camera).

\[ \big\lVert \operatorname{Log}\big(T_{cw} Z_i^{-1}\big)\big\rVert^2_{\Sigma_i} \;\approx\; \big\lVert \operatorname{Log}\big(T^0_{cw} Z_i^{-1}\big) - H_i\,\xi\big\rVert^2_{\Sigma_i} \]

The book perturbs on the right. Perturbing on the left, \(\operatorname{Exp}(\xi)T^0\), is equally valid as long as you stay consistent (footnote 1, p. 57).

Uncertainty: put the Gaussian in the tangent space

In plain words

For a vector we write “state = mean + noise”, \(x = \mu + \delta\). For a rotation that breaks again, because \(R + \text{noise}\) isn't a rotation. The fix is the same trick as before: put the noise on the flat tangent space and wrap it on with Exp:

\(T = \bar T\,\operatorname{Exp}(\delta), \quad \delta \sim \mathcal N(0, \Sigma)\)

Why it matters: picture a robot that drives straight ahead but isn't sure of its heading. The cloud of places it might be bends into a banana. A Gaussian in \(x, y\) can only ever draw an ellipse. A Gaussian in the tangent space, wrapped through Exp, draws the banana naturally (Long et al. [689], Barfoot & Furgale [48]).

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{vector:}\quad & x = \mu + \delta \\ \text{rotation:}\quad & R = \bar R \oplus \delta = \bar R\operatorname{Exp}(\delta) \\ \text{pose:}\quad & T = \bar T \oplus \delta = \bar T\operatorname{Exp}(\delta), \qquad \delta\sim\mathcal N(0,\Sigma) \end{aligned} \]
(2.21–2.23)

RememberOn a Lie group, “mean and covariance” means a mean element \(\bar T\) and a covariance \(\Sigma\) of the tangent-space vector \(\delta\). The covariance a SLAM solver reports for a pose (for example GTSAM's marginalCovariance) is a covariance of \(\delta\), expressed in the pose's own tangent space. It is not a covariance of the 9 or 16 matrix entries. Chapter 6 shows that you can also define distributions directly on rotations.

The toolbox: ⊕, ⊖, inverse, adjoint, Jacobian

In plain words

⊕ “apply a small motion”: \(T_0 \oplus \xi = T_0\operatorname{Exp}(\xi)\). ⊖ “what small motion takes me from \(T_0\) to \(T\)?”: \(T \ominus T_0 = \operatorname{Log}(T_0^{-1}T)\). These two are the Lie-group versions of + and −.

Adjoint “the same motion, described from a different frame”. Turning 10° about the robot's own center is, seen from the world, a turn of 10° about the world origin plus a shift. \(\operatorname{Ad}(T)\) is the matrix that converts one description into the other.

Jacobian \(J\) “rotations don't add, but small ones almost do”. It's the first-order correction that tells you how a small extra rotation changes the axis-angle vector.

\[ \begin{aligned} \xi &= T\ominus T_0 = \operatorname{Log}\!\big(T_0^{-1}T\big) && \text{(difference)} \\ \big(T_0\oplus\xi\big)^{-1} &= (-\xi)\oplus T_0^{-1} && \text{(inverse)} \\ T\operatorname{Exp}(\xi) &= \operatorname{Exp}\!\big(\operatorname{Ad}(T)\,\xi\big)\,T && \text{(adjoint)} \end{aligned} \]
(2.25, 2.26, 2.31)
\[ SE(3)\!: \operatorname{Ad}\!\begin{bmatrix} R & t\\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} R & t^\wedge R \\ 0 & R\end{bmatrix}, \qquad SE(2)\!: \operatorname{Ad}\!\begin{bmatrix} R & t\\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} R & -Jt \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix} \]
(2.30)

Try this
  1. ⊖: drag \(T\) around. The blue curve is \(T_0\operatorname{Exp}(s\xi)\) for \(s\) from 0 to 1, the constant-velocity path from \(T_0\) to \(T\). This exact curve comes back in §2.2.4 as linear interpolation between poses.
  2. ⊖: turn \(T_0\) while leaving \(T\) where it is. \(\xi\) changes, because it is measured in \(T_0\)'s own frame (“forward” means \(T_0\)'s forward).
  3. Adjoint: set ρ = 0 and only turn (θ ≠ 0). In the robot's frame that's a pure spin. In world coordinates, \(\varepsilon = \operatorname{Ad}(T)\xi\) has a non-zero translation part: a spin about the robot equals a spin about the world origin plus a shift. Drag the robot closer to the origin and that shift shrinks.
  4. Adjoint: the two paths \(T\operatorname{Exp}(s\xi)\) (dashed) and \(\operatorname{Exp}(s\varepsilon)T\) (solid) lie exactly on top of each other for every \(s\). As the book stresses, (2.31) holds exactly, with no approximation.

The Jacobian of \(SO(3)\) shows up whenever products of exponentials must be merged. With a small \(\theta_1\):

\[ \operatorname{Exp}(\theta_1)\operatorname{Exp}(\theta_2) \approx \operatorname{Exp}\!\big(\theta_2 + J(\theta_2)^{-1}\theta_1\big), \qquad J(\theta) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{1}{(n+1)!}(\theta^\wedge)^n \]
(2.34–2.35)

It's also behind the kinematics \(\dot\theta = J^{-1}(\theta)\,\omega\) (2.33). Angular velocity \(\omega\) is not simply the time derivative of the axis-angle vector, except near \(\theta = 0\) where \(J \approx I\).

Why continuous time?

In plain words

Chapter 1 added a new pose variable every time a measurement arrived. That's fine for a camera at 10 Hz. It falls apart for a spinning lidar, where each of thousands of points in one sweep is measured at a slightly different moment while the robot keeps moving. You'd need a pose variable per point (huge graph), or you'd pretend all points were taken at once, and the scan comes out bent.

Continuous time says: the robot moves smoothly, so describe its path as a function \(T(t)\) that you can ask “where were you at time \(t\)?” for any \(t\). The function is described by only a few unknowns. Measurement times, estimation variables and query times are now three different things.

There are two families: parametric (splines, next) and nonparametric (Gaussian processes, after that).

Splines: a whole trajectory from a few knobs

In plain words

Pick a fixed row of little “bumps” in time, the basis functions \(\Psi_k(t)\). Give each bump a height knob \(c_k\). The trajectory is the sum of the scaled bumps. The knobs are your unknowns now, not the poses.

The key property is local support: each bump is zero except over a short time window. So a measurement at time \(t_i\) only depends on the few knobs whose bumps cover \(t_i\) (4 for cubic splines). In the factor graph, that measurement is one factor connected to ~4 knob variables (plus a landmark, if any). The graph stays sparse.

\[ p(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{K}\Psi_k(t)\,c_k, \qquad z_i = h_i\!\Big(\sum_k \Psi_k(t_i)c_k,\;\ell\Big) + \eta_i, \qquad \dot p(t) = \sum_k \dot\Psi_k(t)\,c_k \]
(2.36–2.40)

From splines to kernels (§2.2.2)

In plain words

After solving, you care about the trajectory, not about the knobs. Do a little algebra (2.43 → 2.45) and the knobs disappear. What's left is a matrix \(K = \Psi\Psi^\top\) saying “how strongly is the position at time \(t\) tied to the position at time \(t'\)?”. That is a kernel.

The kernel trick: skip the bumps and choose the kernel function \(K(t,t')\) directly. Now you don't have to decide how many bumps to use. The catch: the solve needs \(K^{-1}\), which for a generic kernel is a dense matrix, meaning every pose is tied to every other pose. The next section fixes that.

\[ \begin{aligned} \big(\Psi^\top A^\top A\Psi + I\big)\,\delta_c^* &= \Psi^\top A^\top b && \text{(solve for knobs)} \\ \big(A^\top A + K^{-1}\big)\,\delta^* &= A^\top b, \quad K = \Psi\Psi^\top,\; \delta^* = \Psi\delta_c^* && \text{(same thing, for poses)} \end{aligned} \]
(2.44–2.45)

Compare (2.45) with Chapter 1's normal equations \(A^\top A\,\delta^* = A^\top b\) (1.25): the only new piece is \(K^{-1}\), a prior that rewards smoothness.

Gaussian processes whose inverse kernel is sparse

In plain words

Choose the kernel by describing physics: “the robot roughly keeps its velocity, and its acceleration is random white noise”. This is the constant-velocity prior. The state is position and velocity, \(x(t) = (p(t), v(t))\).

Physics has no memory beyond the current state: to predict the future you only need the state now, not the history (the Markov property). Because of that, \(K^{-1}\) is block-tridiagonal, so the motion prior is just a chain of binary factors between consecutive states, like odometry in Chapter 1. Between those states, the GP tells you exactly how to interpolate, at any time.

\[ \dot x(t) = A\,x(t) + L\,w(t), \qquad w(t)\sim\mathcal{GP}\big(0,\; Q\,\delta(t-t')\big) \]
(2.46)
\[ \text{constant velocity:}\quad \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\dot p\\ \dot v\end{bmatrix}}_{\dot x} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}0 & 1\\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}}_{A}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}p\\ v\end{bmatrix}}_{x} + \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}0\\ 1\end{bmatrix}}_{L} w(t) \]
(2.52)
\[ K = \Phi\,\mathcal Q\,\Phi^\top \quad\Rightarrow\quad K^{-1} = \Phi^{-\top}\mathcal Q^{-1}\Phi^{-1}, \qquad \mathcal Q \text{ block-diagonal} \]
(2.49)
\[ \Phi^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} I \\ -\Phi(t_2,t_1) & I \\ & -\Phi(t_3,t_2) & I \\ && \ddots & \ddots \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{(only two non-zero block diagonals)} \]
(2.51)

For the constant-velocity prior, \(\Phi(t,s) = \begin{bmatrix}1 & t-s\\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}\) and \(Q_k = Q\begin{bmatrix}\Delta^3/3 & \Delta^2/2\\ \Delta^2/2 & \Delta\end{bmatrix}\) with \(\Delta = t_k - t_{k-1}\). Each prior factor is \(\lVert x_k - \Phi(t_k,t_{k-1})\,x_{k-1}\rVert^2_{Q_k}\), meaning “state k should be what constant velocity predicts from state k − 1”. The simpler random-walk prior uses position only, with \(\Phi = 1\) and \(Q_k = Q\Delta\). The book uses it on Lie groups in §2.2.4.

RememberPicking the motion model (an SDE) is picking the kernel. A Markov state makes \(K^{-1}\) block-tridiagonal: one unary factor at the start, then binary factors between neighbours (fig 2.4). Estimating trajectory states plus a map this way is called STEAM (simultaneous trajectory estimation and mapping). After solving, interpolating at any query time costs \(O(1)\), because it only uses the two states on either side.
§2.2.4pp. 69–74eq 2.53–2.75 · fig 2.5–2.7

Splines and GPs on Lie groups

In plain words

A spline is a weighted sum, and poses can't be added. The trick is to rewrite the spline in cumulative form: start at a control point, then add a sequence of differences between neighbouring control points, each scaled by a cumulative weight that ramps from 0 to 1.

Then translate each piece into Lie group language: a sum becomes a product, the difference \(p_k - p_{k-1}\) becomes the relative pose \(T_{k-1}^{-1}T_k\), and scaling a difference by \(\alpha\) becomes \(\operatorname{Exp}(\alpha\operatorname{Log}(\cdot))\), meaning “go fraction \(\alpha\) of the way”.

\[ p(t) = \psi^c_1(t)\,p_1 + \sum_{k=2}^{K}\psi^c_k(t)\,(p_k - p_{k-1}), \qquad \psi^c_k(t) = \sum_{\ell = k}^{K}\psi_\ell(t) \]
(2.54–2.55)
\[ \begin{aligned} T(t) &= \operatorname{Exp}\!\big(\psi^c_k(t)\operatorname{Log}(T_kT_{k-1}^{-1})\big)\,T_{k-1} = \big(T_kT_{k-1}^{-1}\big)^{\alpha_k(t)}\,T_{k-1} \\ \alpha_k(t) &= \frac{t-(k-1)\Delta T}{\Delta T}, \qquad (k-1)\Delta T \le t < k\,\Delta T \end{aligned} \]
(2.61–2.62)

The book writes poses as \(T_{sw}\) (world → sensor) and perturbs on the left. The instrument draws robot poses \(T_{wb} = T_{sw}^{-1}\) and uses the equivalent right-hand form \(T_{k-1}\operatorname{Exp}\big(\alpha\operatorname{Log}(T_{k-1}^{-1}T_k)\big)\). Inverting one formula gives the other, so the curve is identical.

For the GP version, the random-walk prior on the local variable \(\xi_k(t) = \operatorname{Log}(T(t)T_k^{-1})\) gives one motion-prior factor per pair of neighbouring poses. It is just a between-factor that says “expect no relative motion”:

\[ \begin{aligned} e_k &= \operatorname{Log}\!\big(T_kT_{k-1}^{-1}\big), \qquad \text{cost } \lVert e_k\rVert^2_{\Delta T\,Q} \\ e_k &\approx \operatorname{Log}\!\big(T^0_kT^{0\,-1}_{k-1}\big) + \xi_k - \operatorname{Ad}\!\big(T^0_kT^{0\,-1}_{k-1}\big)\,\xi_{k-1} \end{aligned} \]
(2.73, 2.75)

RememberSplines on Lie groups use the cumulative form, with group products in place of sums. GPs on Lie groups use local variables \(\xi_k(t)\) between control poses, so the SDE lives in a vector space again. Both give linear interpolation when the prior or spline is first-order. The big difference (p. 74): the GP approach adds motion-prior factors that regularize the problem, and the plain spline approach doesn't (Johnson et al. [526] compare the two in detail).

The same ideas in GTSAM

GTSAM (github.com/borglab/gtsam) does everything in Part A behind the scenes. Every Pose2, Pose3 and Rot3 is a Lie group element, and the optimizers update them with \(T \leftarrow T\oplus\xi\) internally. Run with pip install gtsam. Watch the tangent-vector ordering: GTSAM's Pose3 puts rotation first, \(\xi = (\theta, \rho)\). The book puts translation first, \(\xi = (\rho, \theta)\).

import numpy as np
import gtsam

# --- §2.1.2 Exp / Log ---------------------------------------------------
R = gtsam.Rot3.Expmap(np.array([0.4, 0.9, 0.6]))     # axis-angle θ ∈ R³ → R ∈ SO(3)   (eq 2.6)
print(gtsam.Rot3.Logmap(R))                           # back to θ

T0 = gtsam.Pose2(1.0, 2.0, 0.5)                       # x, y, heading
xi = np.array([3.0, 0.0, 1.5])                        # Pose2 tangent: [ρ1, ρ2, θ]
T  = T0.compose(gtsam.Pose2.Expmap(xi))               # T0 ⊕ ξ = T0 Exp(ξ)               (eq 2.10)
print(T0.localCoordinates(T))                         # T ⊖ T0 → recovers ξ              (eq 2.25)
print(T0.AdjointMap())                                # Ad(T0)                           (eq 2.30)
print(T0.between(T))                                  # T0⁻¹ T, the relative pose

# --- §2.1.3 optimization on SE(3) --------------------------------------
# A tiny pose graph. Between-factors use the Log error of eq 2.20, and the
# optimizer solves for 6-D tangent steps ξ and retracts them onto SE(3).
graph = gtsam.NonlinearFactorGraph()
prior = gtsam.noiseModel.Diagonal.Sigmas(np.array([0.05] * 3 + [0.1] * 3))  # [rot; trans]
odo   = gtsam.noiseModel.Diagonal.Sigmas(np.array([0.02] * 3 + [0.1] * 3))
step  = gtsam.Pose3(gtsam.Rot3.Yaw(np.pi / 2), gtsam.Point3(2.0, 0.0, 0.0))
graph.add(gtsam.PriorFactorPose3(0, gtsam.Pose3(), prior))
for k in range(4):
    graph.add(gtsam.BetweenFactorPose3(k, k + 1, step, odo))
graph.add(gtsam.BetweenFactorPose3(4, 0, gtsam.Pose3(), odo))   # loop closure

init = gtsam.Values()
for k in range(5):   # a deliberately bad initial guess
    init.insert(k, gtsam.Pose3(gtsam.Rot3.Yaw(1.3 * k), gtsam.Point3(1.5 * k, 0.3 * k, 0.1)))
result = gtsam.LevenbergMarquardtOptimizer(graph, init).optimize()

# --- §2.1.4 uncertainty -------------------------------------------------
# A 6×6 covariance of the tangent-space δ in T = T̄ Exp(δ) (eq 2.23),
# ordered [rotation; translation]. Not a covariance of matrix entries.
print(gtsam.Marginals(graph, result).marginalCovariance(2))

A–ZCheat sheet

Cheat sheet

Manifold
A curved space that looks flat up close, like a circle, a sphere, or the set of all rotations. You can't add points on it, but you can do vector math on the flat tangent space at any point.
Tangent space \(T_\chi\mathcal M\)
The flat vector space touching the manifold at \(\chi\). Its dimension equals the degrees of freedom. Optimization steps and uncertainty live here.
\(SO(d)\)
Rotation matrices: \(R^\top R = I\), \(\det R = 1\). 1 DOF in 2D, 3 in 3D (2.1).
\(SE(d)\)
Pose (transformation) matrices \(\begin{bmatrix}R & t\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\). 3 DOF in 2D, 6 in 3D. Act on homogeneous points \((\ell, 1)\) (2.2–2.3).
Matrix Lie group
A set of matrices that is a smooth manifold and also closed under multiplication, with identity and inverses. Closure means a pose times a pose is a pose.
Lie algebra
The tangent space at the identity, e.g. \(so(3)\) = 3 × 3 skew matrices. A vector space with dimension = DOF.
Hat \(^\wedge\) / vee \(^\vee\)
Hat turns a vector into a Lie algebra matrix (for \(SO(3)\), the cross-product matrix). Vee goes back.
Exp / Log
\(\operatorname{Exp}(\theta) = \exp(\theta^\wedge)\): vector → group. \(\operatorname{Log}(R) = \log(R)^\vee\): group → vector. Exp is onto but not one-to-one (\(2\pi\) wraps).
Axis-angle
The \(SO(3)\) tangent vector: direction = rotation axis, length = angle in radians.
⊕ / ⊖
\(T_0\oplus\xi = T_0\operatorname{Exp}(\xi)\), \(T\ominus T_0 = \operatorname{Log}(T_0^{-1}T)\) (2.24–2.25). The Lie-group versions of + and −.
Perturbation (right / left)
\(T = T^0\operatorname{Exp}(\xi)\) (right, body frame) or \(\operatorname{Exp}(\xi)T^0\) (left, world frame). Pick one and use it consistently.
\(\odot\) operator
\(\xi^\wedge\tilde\ell = \tilde\ell^\odot\xi\), with \(\tilde\ell^\odot = \begin{bmatrix}I & -\ell^\wedge\\0&0\end{bmatrix}\) (2.15). Moves the unknown to the right so least squares can see it.
Retraction
Any map that takes a tangent step back onto the manifold. Exp is one, normalization or Cayley are others. Riemannian optimization = solve on the tangent space, then retract.
Gaussian on a Lie group
\(T = \bar T\operatorname{Exp}(\delta)\), \(\delta\sim\mathcal N(0,\Sigma)\) (2.23). It makes banana-shaped clouds in \(x, y\).
Adjoint \(\operatorname{Ad}(T)\)
Re-expresses a tangent vector from \(T\)'s frame in the world frame: \(T\operatorname{Exp}(\xi) = \operatorname{Exp}(\operatorname{Ad}(T)\xi)T\), exactly (2.31). For \(SE(3)\): \(\begin{bmatrix}R & t^\wedge R\\0 & R\end{bmatrix}\).
Left Jacobian \(J(\theta)\)
\(\sum \frac{1}{(n+1)!}(\theta^\wedge)^n\). Gives \(\operatorname{Exp}(\theta_1)\operatorname{Exp}(\theta_2)\approx\operatorname{Exp}(\theta_2 + J(\theta_2)^{-1}\theta_1)\) and \(\dot\theta = J^{-1}\omega\) (2.33–2.35).
Continuous-time trajectory
A function \(p(t)\) or \(T(t)\) that you can query at any time. Measurement times, estimation variables and query times are separate things.
Spline / basis functions
\(p(t) = \sum_k\Psi_k(t)c_k\) with locally supported \(\Psi_k\). A measurement becomes a factor on the few active coefficients (2.36–2.39).
Cumulative basis
\(\psi^c_k = \sum_{\ell\ge k}\psi_\ell\). Rewrites a spline as start point + scaled differences, which is the form that carries over to Lie groups (2.54–2.62).
Kernel \(K(t,t')\)
Prior covariance between the states at two times. The kernel trick picks it directly instead of choosing basis functions (2.45).
Gaussian process (GP)
A distribution over functions, defined by a mean and a kernel. Here: a trajectory prior, derived from an SDE driven by white noise (2.46–2.48).
Constant-velocity prior
White-noise acceleration, with state \((p, v)\). The SDE is (2.52). Its \(K^{-1}\) is block-tridiagonal, so the prior is a chain of binary factors.
Random-walk prior
White-noise velocity, with position-only state, \(\Phi = I\). Its interpolation is linear. On \(SE(d)\) its factors are \(\lVert\operatorname{Log}(T_kT_{k-1}^{-1})\rVert^2\) (2.73).
STEAM
Simultaneous trajectory estimation and mapping: SLAM with a continuous-time GP trajectory.
